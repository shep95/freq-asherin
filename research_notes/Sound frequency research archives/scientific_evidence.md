# Scientific Evidence Check: Sound Frequencies Used in shepherd.freq

Scope: peer-reviewed and official evidence for each frequency or technique family the app uses. Evidence labels are **Strong / Moderate / Weak / None**. Research current to 2026-10-25. Where I relied on a secondary summary rather than the primary paper, the finding says so.

## 1. Binaural beats and isochronic tones (cognition, anxiety, pain, sleep, EEG entrainment)

### Takeaway
Binaural beats show a small-to-medium pooled behavioural effect on memory, attention, anxiety and pain in one 2019 meta-analysis (g = 0.45). Later reviews (2023–2026) find the EEG "entrainment" mechanism inconsistent and the trial evidence mostly low quality. Isochronic tones have hardly been studied. Evidence label: **Weak to moderate** for short-term anxiety and acute-pain effects, **weak/mixed** for cognition, **weak/none** for sleep, and **mixed/unsupported** for brainwave entrainment.

### Cited Findings
- Garcia-Argibay, Santed & Reales (2019), *Psychological Research* 83(2):357–372, meta-analysis: 22 studies and 35 effect sizes, with an overall effect of g = 0.45 (medium, significant, consistent) on memory, attention, anxiety and analgesia. Masking with white or pink noise did not appear necessary. — [PubMed 30073406](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30073406/)
- In the same meta-analysis, exposure before the task (or before and during it) worked better than exposure only during the task, and longer exposure predicted larger effects. — [Univ. of Southampton repository](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/488754)
- The 2019 meta-analysis did not pool sleep outcomes. Commercial sites that cite g = 0.45 as proof of sleep benefit go beyond what it tested. — [PubMed 30073406](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30073406/) (scope as stated in the abstract)
- Ingendoh et al. (2023), *PLOS ONE*, systematic review of EEG entrainment: of 14 studies, 5 supported the entrainment hypothesis, 8 contradicted it and 1 was mixed. The studies were "very heterogeneous" in how the stimulation was delivered. — [PMC10198548](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10198548/)
- Shamsi et al. (2024), *BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies*, systematic review of RCTs on pain: the studies were too heterogeneous to pool, most had high risk of bias, and evidence quality was low to very low. The authors see a possible benefit for acute pain and say chronic pain needs better trials. — [BMC CMT 2024](https://bmccomplementmedtherapies.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12906-024-04339-y); [IASP Pain Research Forum](https://www.iasp-pain.org/publications/pain-research-forum/papers-of-the-week/paper/does-brain-entrainment-using-binaural-auditory-beats-affect-pain-perception-in-acute-and-chronic-pain-a-systematic-review/)
- A later meta-analysis in *Psychological Research* on memory and attention reported mixed results. I did not retrieve its pooled effect sizes. — reported in search summary; PubMed record appearing in results: [PubMed 35842538](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35842538/) (identity and contents not independently verified)
- Elnazer (published Feb 2026, Cambridge, PROSPERO-registered) reviewed music and binaural-beat interventions for anxiety, sleep and cognition in young adults. Of 122 abstracts screened, 10 trials were included. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4282E1152FD3EACA5DF8304935165202)
- Isochronic tones: a review in *Revista Mexicana de Neurociencia* (2021) kept 17 higher-quality randomized trials out of 674 screened records. 15 tested binaural beats and only 2 (11.76%) tested isochronic tones. — [Rev Mex Neurocienc PDF](https://www.scielo.org.mx/pdf/rmn/v22n6/2604-6180-rmn-22-6-238.pdf)
- Isochronic tones are "commercially marketed as effective in brainwave entrainment" but have had "limited empirical investigation". — [Inquiries Journal 2020](https://www.inquiriesjournal.com/articles/1830/are-isochronic-tones-effective-the-impact-of-isochronic-tones-on-brainwave-entrainment-and-stress)
- Auditory steady-state responses (ASSRs) to amplitude-modulated tones at about 10–70 Hz are a real, established measure used in audiometry. This shows the auditory cortex follows modulation rates. It does not show that the brain's overall state changes. — [ABE 2013 review](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/abe/2/0/2_55/_article)

### Inferences
- The defensible claim for the app is narrow: binaural beats *may* modestly reduce state anxiety and acute pain perception, and may slightly help some attention or memory tasks. Effects are small, the studies vary widely, and risk of bias is high.
- "Entrains your brain to theta/delta" is not supported as a reliable mechanism. The EEG evidence is split roughly 5 to 8 against.
- Isochronic tones reliably produce a cortical ASSR, which is a measurable "following" response. There is almost no RCT evidence that they produce psychological benefits.

### Gaps
- I could not verify a widely cited 2025 meta-analysis of 15 RCTs in *Complementary Therapies in Medicine* that reportedly tracked adverse events. I only saw it on a vendor blog (Brain.fm).
- I found no Cochrane review on binaural beats.
- I did not retrieve pooled sleep-specific RCT data.

## 2. 40 Hz gamma sensory stimulation (GENUS)

### Takeaway
The MIT Tsai lab's mouse findings (2016 onward) are striking but contested. A 2023 study by Soula et al. (Buzsáki lab) could not reproduce the amyloid reduction or robust gamma entrainment. Human evidence comes from small feasibility trials. The pivotal HOPE trial (NCT05637801, n = 673) completed in August 2026, but I found no published topline result as of late October 2026. Evidence label: **Moderate** in animals (contested) and **weak/preliminary** in humans. For a sound-only consumer app (no synchronized light, no medical device), **none**.

### Cited Findings
- Iaccarino et al. (2016) reported large amyloid and plaque reductions in 5xFAD mice after one hour of 40 Hz light flicker. Soula et al. (2023) ran experiments in 5xFAD mice and **did not reproduce** this. They found plaques were not changed in two mouse lines and 40 Hz light did not produce robust gamma rhythms in visual cortex. The mice also actively avoided the stimulus. — [Alzforum: "Does Flashing Light Really Lower Cortical Amyloid?"](https://alzforum.org/node/2701666); [Alzforum paper listing](https://alzforum.org/papers/forty-hertz-light-stimulation-does-not-entrain-native-gamma-oscillations-alzheimers-disease)
- Reactions were divided. Mander said the result casts some doubt on the approach. Tsai's group argued that native gamma and evoked 40 Hz oscillations are distinct, so the therapy is not majorly threatened. Commenters attributed the discrepancy to larger animal numbers in the Soula work and to methodological differences. — [Alzforum](https://alzforum.org/node/2701666)
- Tsai lab, 2024 *Nature*: 40 Hz multisensory stimulation was reported to clear amyloid through a glymphatic mechanism in mice. — [ScienceDaily, Feb 2024](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/02/240228114328.htm); [MIT Media Lab](https://www-prod.media.mit.edu/articles/how-sensory-gamma-rhythm-stimulation-clears-amyloid-in-alzheimer-s-mice/)
- Human feasibility (OVERTURE, Cognito Therapeutics): a small trial reporting safety and tolerability plus efficacy *estimates* in mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease. Reported slowing of decline and reduced atrophy came largely from post hoc analyses. — [PMC10957179](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10957179/); [NeurologyLive](https://www.neurologylive.com/view/cognito-gamma-sensory-stimulation-leads-preserved-corpus-callosum-structure-alzheimers)
- HOPE pivotal trial (NCT05637801): randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled, about 70 US sites, 12-month treatment plus 12-month open-label extension. The Spectris device delivers **synchronized 40 Hz light and sound**. The primary endpoint is a composite of MMSE and ADCS-ADL. MMSE range is 15–28. Actual enrollment was 673. Primary completion was 22 Jun 2026, study completion 17 Aug 2026, and the registry was last updated 9 Sep 2026. — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05637801](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05637801); [BioSpace enrollment release](https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/cognito-therapeutics-completes-enrollment-in-hope-pivotal-study-of-spectris-ad-therapy-for-the-treatment-of-patients-with-alzheimers-disease); [BrightFocus update](https://www.brightfocus.org/resource/non-invasive-light-and-sound-stimulation-therapy-in-alzheimers-update-on-hope-study/)
- Spectris has FDA Breakthrough Device Designation and remains investigational, not commercially available. The company had guided to topline data in mid-2026. — [MedTech Spectrum, Feb 2026](https://medtechspectrum.com/news/2/24493/cognito-therapeutics-completes-enrollment-in-hope-pivotal-trial-for-spectris-ad-therapy.html); [Business Wire, Jun 2025](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250611320901/en)
- Company materials cite "up to 76% reduction in cognitive decline" from OVERTURE. This is a promotional figure from a small, non-pivotal study. — [Silicon UK press release](https://www.silicon.co.uk/press-release/cognito-therapeutics-announces-non-invasive-neuromodulation-therapy-delays-alzheimers-disease-progression)

### Inferences
- Even if HOPE succeeds, the result would apply to a specific medical device delivering combined light and sound with controlled parameters and daily, supervised use over 12 months. It would not validate a 40 Hz tone played from a phone.
- The replication dispute means even the animal mechanism is not settled.

### Gaps
- **HOPE topline results**: none were found in searches as of 2026-10-25, even though the trial completed in August 2026. Re-check Cognito press releases and the ClinicalTrials.gov results tab before the report goes out.
- I could not confirm from the primary source whether Soula et al. was published in *Nature Neuroscience*. Alzforum coverage confirms the study and its findings.

## 3. Infrasound (wind turbines, Tandy 18.98 Hz "ghost" frequency)

### Takeaway
The best controlled evidence is a 2023 double-blind University of Sydney trial with 72-hour exposures. It found no adverse physiological or psychological effects from simulated wind-turbine infrasound. Australia's NHMRC (2015) found no consistent evidence that wind farms harm health. Vic Tandy's 18.98 Hz work consists of two case reports in a parapsychology journal and has not been replicated under controlled conditions. Evidence label: **moderate** that real-world infrasound at wind-farm levels is not harmful; **weak/none** for Tandy-style "fear/apparition" effects. Consumer speakers also cannot reproduce meaningful infrasound SPL (see Inferences).

### Cited Findings
- University of Sydney / Woolcock Institute (Marshall et al., 2023, *Environmental Health Perspectives*): 37 adults (19 male, 18 female) spent three 72-hour periods in sleep-lab "apartments". Each period was simulated wind-turbine infrasound, traffic noise or sham, in random order and double-blind. There was "no evidence" of adverse physiological or psychological effects from infrasound. Traffic noise increased wake time after sleep onset by about 6 minutes versus sham; infrasound did not. The authors acknowledge it is one relatively small study. — [RenewEconomy](https://reneweconomy.com.au/wind-farm-noise-not-harmful-to-health-new-study-finds-no-evidence-of-wind-turbine-syndrome/); [PMC10174642](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10174642)
- A Flinders University study (68 participants, 7 nights) also reported no discernible sleep effect from infrasound. As of the coverage, it was available as a 2023 conference paper and not yet in a peer-reviewed journal. — [RenewEconomy](https://reneweconomy.com.au/scientific-dead-end-the-new-push-to-get-wind-turbine-syndrome-back-on-the-radar/)
- NHMRC Statement (Feb 2015): "no consistent evidence that wind farms cause adverse health effects in humans". More than 4,000 papers were screened and only 13 were of sufficient quality. The statement called for more high-quality research and launched a targeted research call. — [NHMRC wind farms page](https://nhmrc.gov.au/health-advice/environmental-health/wind-farms); [NHMRC Information Paper EH57A](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/reports/wind-farm-info-paper-eh57a.pdf); [Univ. of Adelaide systematic review](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/89c2b5c1-e482-47f5-bba7-bcd814047229/full)
- Tandy & Lawrence (1998), "The Ghost in the Machine," *J. Society for Psychical Research* 62:360–364: an extractor fan in a lab produced a standing wave measured at 18.98 Hz, which Tandy linked to feelings of unease and peripheral "apparitions". — [Skeptic's Dictionary](https://skepdic.com/infrasound.html); [Acoustics Today 2012](https://acousticstoday.org/issues/2012AT/Apr2012/files/basic-html/page55.html)
- Tandy (2000), "Something in the Cellar," *JSPR* 64(860): reported infrasound at the same predicted frequency where apparitions had been reported in a 14th-century Coventry cellar. — [PDF of paper](https://last.hit.bme.hu/download/AOAB/Infrahang_irodalom/Something-in-the-Cellar.pdf)

### Inferences
- Tandy's papers are two uncontrolled field case reports with no blinding. They are hypothesis-generating at best. Calling 18.98 Hz a "fear frequency" overstates them.
- Phone and laptop speakers, and most headphones, have little or no output below about 20–40 Hz. An app "playing 7 Hz or 19 Hz" mostly produces harmonics or nothing audible, not a true infrasonic field at the levels studied (wind-farm or Tandy levels). This is general acoustics, not drawn from a cited source.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled replication of the 18.98 Hz apparition effect. The 2003 "Soundless Music" concert experiment (Wiseman, Lord et al.) is often cited, but I did not retrieve a source in this pass.
- I did not retrieve the Sydney trial's exact infrasound SPL from the primary paper.

## 4. Solfeggio frequencies (incl. 528 Hz) and 432 Hz tuning

### Takeaway
The only controlled studies are tiny pilots, often co-authored by people with an interest in alternative tuning, published in low-impact journals, and not replicated. Evidence label: **weak** for any 432 Hz versus 440 Hz difference and **weak/none** for 528 Hz "DNA repair/healing" or other Solfeggio claims. No systematic review supports them.

### Cited Findings
- Calamassi, Lucicesare, Pomponi & Bambi (2020), *Acta Biomedica*: a double-blind crossover pilot with 12 patients with spinal cord injury, comparing their favourite music tuned to 432 Hz or 440 Hz. Sleep scores improved after 432 Hz but not after 440 Hz, and the authors called for larger studies. Pomponi is an independent musician and sound engineer. — [Acta Biomedica 10755](https://mattioli1885journals.com/index.php/actabiomedica/article/view/10755)
- A 2022 randomized pilot in emergency nurses during COVID-19 compared 440 Hz and 432 Hz music for anxiety and stress, again with Pomponi as co-author. — [Acta Biomedica 12915](https://mattioli1885journals.com/index.php/actabiomedica/article/view/12915)
- A Calamassi & Pomponi (2019) item on 432 Hz is cited in later theses (published in *Explore*, a small crossover study in healthy volunteers). Retuning to 432 Hz is usually done by slowing the original track. — [Univ. Padova thesis](https://thesis.unipd.it/bitstream/20.500.12608/59910/1/Marmolaro%20Mattia.pdf)
- Akimoto et al. (2018), *Health* (SCIRP): 9 healthy adults (1 man, 8 women) heard 528 Hz and 440 Hz music on separate days. After 528 Hz, cortisol fell and oxytocin rose significantly. LF/HF ratio fell after both. — [SCIRP full text](https://www.scirp.org/html/2-8204397_87146.htm)
- Popular summaries overstate Akimoto (2018), for example claiming proof of "parasympathetic dominance". — [scienceinsights.org](https://scienceinsights.org/what-is-528-hz-good-for-benefits-and-claims-explained/); [Northern Light Health](https://northernlighthealth.org/Wellness/Rocks-Water/Posts/March,-2023/The-Healing-Frequency)

### Inferences
- Retuning music from 440 to 432 Hz shifts pitch by about 32 cents (less than a third of a semitone). Slowing the track also changes tempo. Any effect could come from tempo, familiarity or expectation rather than "frequency", and no study controls for all of these.
- Scientific Research Publishing (SCIRP), the publisher of Akimoto 2018, has been widely criticised for weak peer review. This lowers confidence further.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT larger than about 30 participants, no meta-analysis, and no independent replication for 432 or 528 Hz.
- I found no primary-literature support for the claim that "528 Hz repairs DNA".

## 5. Schumann resonances

### Takeaway
Schumann resonances are real. They are extremely-low-frequency *electromagnetic* resonances of the Earth–ionosphere cavity (fundamental about 7.83 Hz). Claims of biological entrainment rest on correlational or theoretical papers, and there are no controlled human trials. An audio tone at 7.83 Hz is a pressure wave, not an EM field, and is below the range most speakers can reproduce. Evidence label: physics **strong**; biological or entrainment effects **weak/none**; audio "Schumann" tones **none**.

### Cited Findings
- 2025 review in *Electromagnetic Biology and Medicine*: proposes that ELF fields including the 7.83 Hz Schumann fundamental *may* influence calcium signalling and membrane potential. It notes correlations between atmospheric fields and EEG and says mechanisms need further study before health implications are understood. — [PubMed 40394813](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40394813/)
- Nevoit et al. (2025), *Applied Sciences* (MDPI): a theoretical review and literature synthesis on Schumann resonance and health, not a clinical trial. — [MDPI 2076-3417/15/1/449](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/1/449)
- Wellness sources claiming Schumann exposure reduces stress or improves sleep or cognition give no primary citations. — [Aura Health blog](https://aurahealth.io/blog/exploring-the-schumann-resonance-a-guide-to-earths-natural-frequency)

### Inferences
- The natural Schumann field strength is on the order of picotesla. Human EEG alpha (8–12 Hz) overlapping 7.83 Hz numerically is coincidence and not evidence of coupling. This is general physics, not from a cited source.
- Playing 7.83 Hz as sound is a category error: it is acoustic, not electromagnetic. It is also infrasonic, so headphones will barely reproduce it. A 7.83 Hz binaural *beat* (for example 200 Hz and 207.83 Hz) is just a binaural beat (see section 1).

### Gaps
- I found no RCTs of Schumann-frequency EM or audio exposure on any clinical outcome.

## 6. High-frequency sound (>15–20 kHz) and airborne ultrasound

### Takeaway
Leighton (Southampton, 2016–2018) documented unrecognised public exposure to very-high-frequency and ultrasonic sound from PA systems, pest repellers and similar sources. He linked it to complaints of nausea, dizziness, migraine, fatigue and tinnitus, but he explicitly says the evidence is insufficient to confirm or deny causation. Exposure guidelines are old and occupational, and largely exclude young people. The Mosquito anti-loitering device (about 17.4 kHz) is designed to be heard mostly by people under about 25 because high-frequency hearing declines with age. Evidence label: exposure occurs, **strong**; symptoms at public levels, **weak** (plausible, not established); audibility declining with age, **strong**.

### Cited Findings
- Leighton (2016), *Proc. R. Soc. A*, "Are some people suffering as a result of increasing mass exposure of the public to ultrasound in air?": found exposures in railway stations, museums, libraries, schools and stadiums, with complaints of nausea, dizziness, migraine, fatigue and tinnitus. Guidelines are inadequate for public exposure and rest on evidence mostly collected over 40 years ago. There has been insufficient research to confirm or deny a link. — [Royal Soc A paper PDF](https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/the-sounds-of-silence-might-be-making-us-suffer/Ultrasound-in-the-air_Royal-Soc-A_Paper.pdf); [Southampton news 2016](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/engineering/news/2016/01/20-tim-leighton-ultrasound.page); [eprint 385213](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/385213)
- Fletcher et al. (2018), *JASA*: measured SPLs from 16 publicly available or installed sources, including PA/voice-alarm systems and pest deterrents. — [eprint 418770](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/418770); [Southampton project page](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/engineering/research/projects/are-some-people-suffering.page)
- Leighton (2018), *JASA* editorial: a member of the public can buy a $20 device that exposes others to SPLs more than 50 dB above public Maximum Permissible Levels. No data from young people were used when those limits were set. — [JASA editorial PDF](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/423687/1/2018_Leighton_JASA_Editorial_Ultrasound_in_air_version_post_review.pdf)
- Mosquito device: the current version has a roughly 17.4 kHz setting "generally heard only by young people". The manufacturer says it is typically audible under 25. Individual variation exists: some people over 25 hear it and some teens do not. — [Wikipedia: The Mosquito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mosquito); [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/mosquito-devices-may-be-used-to-disperse-australian-delinquents-but-how-do-they-work-14743); [Language Log](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=43506)
- Teens repurposed the tone as "Teen Buzz/Mosquito" ringtones, sometimes at about 14.4 kHz. — [Sound & Vision](https://www.soundandvision.com/content/reality-bytes-mosquito-ringtones)

### Inferences
- An app tone at 15–20 kHz can be inaudible to an adult user yet clearly audible and annoying to children or pets nearby. Users may also turn the volume up to "hear" an inaudible tone. Both are practical safety issues.
- Most phone DACs and speakers roll off near 20 kHz, and standard 44.1/48 kHz sample rates cap output at 22–24 kHz. A consumer app cannot emit meaningful "ultrasound" above about 22 kHz.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled double-blind provocation trial at public-space ultrasound levels in this pass.

## 7. Hearing safety (WHO / NIOSH / pure tones)

### Takeaway
NIOSH sets a recommended exposure limit of 85 dB(A) as an 8-hour time-weighted average with a 3 dB exchange rate. The WHO/ITU safe-listening standard for personal audio is 80 dB(A) for 40 hours a week for adults, and 75 dB(A) for 40 hours a week for children or sensitive users. OSHA's legal limit is looser (90 dBA, 5 dB exchange). Evidence label: **strong** (official consensus standards).

### Cited Findings
- NIOSH REL: 85 dB(A) based on an 8-hour workday. — [CDC Stacks document](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/128772/cdc_128772_DS1.pdf); [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/212792/cdc_212792_DS1.pdf)
- 3 dB exchange rate: each 3 dB increase halves the allowed time. 85 dB for 40 h/week is equivalent to 88 dB for 20 h and 91 dB for 10 h. — [Canadian Academy of Audiology fact sheet](https://canadianaudiology.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/CAA_Fast-Facts_Hearing-and-streaming-music-from-our-phones.pdf)
- WHO/ITU H.870 safe listening (Make Listening Safe): 80 dB(A) for 40 h/week for adults, and 75 dB(A) for 40 h/week for children or sensitive users. — [PMC10194210](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10194210/); [Canadian Audiologist](https://canadianaudiologist.ca/safe-listening/?output=pdf)
- OSHA PEL: 90 dBA over 8 h with a 5 dB exchange rate. — [Bellman summary](https://ca.bellman.com/blogs/news/how-loud-is-too-loud-safe-decibel-levels)
- Conflict: some consumer calculators misstate that WHO also sets "85 dB for 8 h". WHO's personal-audio figure is a weekly 80 dB(A) dose. — [theaudiostuff calculator](https://theaudiostuff.com/tools/safe-listening-calculator/) (incorrect framing)

### Inferences
- Considerations specific to pure tones: dB(A) weighting underweights low frequencies, so a low tone can carry high SPL while reading "quieter" on an A-weighted meter. Sustained pure tones concentrate energy at one cochlear place, and continuous tones are more fatiguing and annoying than broadband music. A sudden onset at high volume can startle or cause a transient threshold shift. The app should cap default volume, fade tones in, and show a sound-dose warning. These are design inferences, not from a cited guideline.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the primary WHO "Make Listening Safe" or ITU-T H.870 text. Figures come from peer-reviewed and audiology-association secondary sources.
- I found no official limit written specifically for pure tones.

## 8. Focused ultrasound neuromodulation (medical, transcranial)

### Takeaway
MR-guided **high-intensity** focused ultrasound (Insightec Exablate Neuro) is FDA-approved, but it works by **thermal ablation** (destroying a small target) for medication-refractory essential tremor and tremor-dominant Parkinson's disease. It is not a "frequency" therapy. **Low-intensity** transcranial focused ultrasound for neuromodulation (mood, pain, cognition) remains investigational. A phone or speaker cannot do any of this. Evidence label: approved ablation for tremor, **strong**; low-intensity neuromodulation, **research only**; consumer audio as "ultrasound", **none**.

### Cited Findings
- FDA approved Exablate Neuro for essential tremor patients who have not responded to medication. In the pivotal trial (76 participants, 56 treated and 20 sham), the treated group improved 46.9% on the CRST score at 3 months and maintained about 40% improvement at 1 year. — [Practical Neurology](https://practicalneurology.com/news/the-fda-has-approved-a-focused-ultrasound-device-for-essential-tremor/2475661/); [MDedge](https://mdedge.com/content/focused-ultrasound-device-approved-treat-essential-tremor)
- FDA labeling (P150038 supplement) covers medication-refractory tremor in tremor-dominant idiopathic Parkinson's disease. The device "uses high intensity focused ultrasound to thermally ablate the target tissue". — [FDA accessdata P150038S006D](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf15/P150038S006D.pdf)
- Sources disagree on the date of the broader Parkinson's indication: a 2021 approval for drug-refractory Parkinson's per Practical Neurology, versus a 2025 approval for severe Parkinson's motor symptoms per BioWorld. — [Practical Neurology](https://practicalneurology.com/news/the-fda-has-approved-a-focused-ultrasound-device-for-essential-tremor/2475661/); contrasted with [BioWorld](https://www.bioworld.com/keywords/19645-exablate-neuro)
- FDA granted an IDE for a pivotal study of staged bilateral focused-ultrasound treatment for essential tremor, showing that further indications are still in trials. — [Practical Neurology](https://practicalneurology.com/news/fda-allows-study-of-bilateral-ultrasound-treatment-of-essential-tremor/2469492/); [MassDevice](https://www.massdevice.com/news/fda-approves-brain-ultrasound-study-with-insightecs-exablate-neuro)

### Inferences (physics; not from cited sources)
- Therapeutic transcranial focused ultrasound uses MHz-range frequencies (about 0.2–0.7 MHz for transcranial work) from phased-array transducers coupled to the scalp through water or gel. Many elements are corrected for skull aberration to form a millimetre-scale focus.
- Consumer speakers top out near 20–24 kHz (set by the sample rate). They radiate into air, and almost all airborne sound reflects off the skin and skull because of the large air-to-tissue impedance mismatch. They cannot focus. So "ultrasound neuromodulation via an app" is physically impossible.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve current (2025–2026) status of low-intensity TUS trials, for example in depression or addiction, or confirm the original essential-tremor approval year (widely reported as 2016) from a primary FDA page.

## 9. PEMF (pulsed electromagnetic field therapy)

### Takeaway
PEMF bone-growth stimulators have been FDA-approved through premarket approval since 1979. Indications are non-union fractures, failed fusions, congenital pseudarthrosis and adjunct to lumbar or cervical spinal fusion. Clearances often claimed for depression or brain tumours could not be verified here. Audio at the same Hz is a pressure wave in air, not a magnetic field inducing currents in tissue, so it is **not** PEMF. Evidence label: PEMF for bone non-union, **moderate/strong (regulatory)**; audio as PEMF, **none**.

### Cited Findings
- FDA first approved a PEMF bone-growth stimulator in November 1979. About six devices have since gone through PMA. Indications include non-union fractures, failed fusions, congenital pseudarthrosis and adjunct to spinal fusion. — [scienceinsights.org](https://scienceinsights.org/what-is-pemf-therapy-benefits-uses-and-limits/)
- In 2004 a PEMF system was approved as an adjunct to cervical fusion surgery in patients at high risk of non-fusion. In 2013 FDA warned a manufacturer for promoting PEMF for unapproved uses such as cerebral palsy and spinal cord injury. — [Wikipedia: PEMF](https://www.wikipedia.com/wiki/PEMF)
- Wellness sites claim later PEMF clearances for depression and certain brain tumours. These could not be confirmed from an FDA source, and Wikipedia describes depression as a research use. — [Outliyr](https://outliyr.com/reports/pemf-therapy) (unverified claim)
- Peer-reviewed PEMF overview. — [PMC5822965](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5822965)

### Inferences
- PEMF works by Faraday induction: a time-varying magnetic field from a coil induces electric currents in tissue. A speaker's coil field is tiny and confined to the driver, and what reaches the user is acoustic pressure. A "PEMF frequency" played as sound has no PEMF mechanism.
- The tumour device people may be thinking of is likely Tumor Treating Fields (Optune). That uses electric fields at about 200 kHz and is not PEMF or sound. This comes from background knowledge and was not verified in this pass.

### Gaps
- I did not query the FDA PMA/510(k) database directly. Confirmations come from secondary sources.

## 10. Shattering glass with voice or sound

### Takeaway
Shattering is real but hard. It requires a thin, resonant (typically lead-crystal) glass with a flaw, a tone matched to its resonant frequency (around 500–1,000 Hz), sustained at roughly 100–105+ dB at close range. MythBusters (2005) had singer Jamie Vendera shatter a wineglass unamplified at about 556 Hz and about 105 dB, after about 12–20 attempts. Evidence label: **strong** that it is physically possible (documented demonstration); phone speakers are **very unlikely** to achieve it.

### Cited Findings
- MythBusters (2005): Jamie Vendera shattered a wineglass with unamplified voice at 556 Hz and about 105 dB after about 20 attempts. — [Mental Floss](https://mentalfloss.com/article/20994/can-opera-singers-really-shatter-glass); [Wonderopolis](https://wonderopolis.org/wonder/How-Can-a-Voice-Break-Glass)
- Scientific American says he tried 12 glasses before one broke, which differs from Mental Floss's 20 attempts. Only "the finest leaded crystal" is resonant enough to break at volumes some people can produce, "upward of 100 decibels". A materials engineer quoted: "It's possible, but you have to be both good and lucky." — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/fact-or-fiction-opera-singer-can-shatter-glass/)
- NPR's transcript quotes the claim that Vendera can sing above 120 dB. That describes his general capability, not necessarily the level at shatter. — [NPR transcript](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/10255528)
- The mechanism is resonance, where sustained energy at the glass's natural frequency builds vibration amplitude until it fractures. — [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/37005-singer-break-glass-singing-break-wineglass.html); [Science Notes](https://sciencenotes.org/break-glass-voice/)

### Inferences
- A phone speaker produces roughly 80–90 dB at 10 cm and is not directed at the glass. Hitting the glass's exact resonance (which first has to be measured by tapping) while staying in the 100+ dB range is unrealistic without an amplifier and a driver held close. Classroom demos use amplified drivers. The app should not imply its tones can break glass.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed engineering paper giving SPL thresholds versus glass type. Figures come from science journalism.
